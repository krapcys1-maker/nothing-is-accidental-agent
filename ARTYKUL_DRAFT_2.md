# The Yogurt Is Fine Two Days After The Date, Or It Isn't, And A Company Decided Which

On the lid of a yogurt pot there is a date. In front of that date sits one of two phrases, and the choice between them is not a matter of house style.

If the pot says *best before*, then food sold after that date is not a breach of food safety law. Best before dates are advisory, and they relate only to the quality of the food. If the pot says *use by*, the Food Standards Agency's line is blunt: a use-by date is about safety, it is the most important date to remember, and foods can be eaten until the use-by date but not after.

Same chilled cabinet, two different legal worlds. Which means the interesting question is not whether the yogurt is fine on Thursday when the pack said Tuesday. The interesting question is who decided which of those two phrases to print, and what they had to have in a drawer before they were allowed to print it.

The answer is the mechanism most people walk past. UK law supplies the two categories. It does not sort products into them. That sorting is done by the food business operator, the company that makes or sells the food, before the product goes to market, on the basis of the product type, its ingredients and how it has been processed. The regulator sets the meaning of the words. The producer decides which word applies to this particular pot.

That is a delegation, and delegations come with homework. The operator's responsibility is to carry out adequate testing for each new product to establish its microbiological safety time limits, which is the technical way of saying: the point at which harmful bacteria stop being a theoretical presence and become a hazard. The output of that testing is a durability date, the date actually printed on the pack. If the testing shows no safety risk arises after that point, the pack gets a best before. If it does, the pack gets a use by, and selling the product after that date stops being a commercial decision and becomes a legal one.

The homework does not finish at launch. The same responsibility extends to monitoring consumer complaints about the food, so that the testing and analysis data stays robust and the durability date stays correct. A complaint pattern is a signal that the file is out of date. In principle, then, a shelf life is not a guess dressed up as a number. It is an evidence file that has to remain defensible for as long as the product is on sale.

It is also, by construction, conditional. A date produced by testing is a statement about a product held in particular conditions, which is why the storage instruction sits next to the date rather than somewhere decorative on the back of the pack. The number answers a narrow question: under these conditions, until when. It does not answer the broader question consumers actually ask it, which is whether this food is any good.

One further feature makes the delegation durable rather than occasional. Guidance in this area, such as WRAP's, covers not just how to apply a date label but how UK law applies across the whole life cycle of a food product, from initial design and development right through to sale or redistribution. The date is not something stamped on at the end of the line. It is a design parameter, decided alongside the recipe and the process, and it travels with the product to the point where someone decides whether it can still be sold or given away.

Now the incentives, in the simplest form I can state them honestly. Printing *use by* makes selling after the date illegal, so it shortens the product's saleable life and hands the retailer a hard deadline. Printing *best before* keeps the product legally saleable past the date, but only if the operator can stand behind the finding that no safety risk appears once the date has passed. So the classification is a trade: the more generous category demands the more confident evidence. My reading is that this puts a quiet thumb on the scale. Caution is cheap for the firm and expensive for everyone downstream, because the cost of an over-tight date does not land on the company that set it. It lands on the shopper who reads the number as a property of the food, and on whoever handles what does not sell.

Which brings the strongest objection, and it is a real one. If operators classify their own products, then two near-identical items can carry different categories, and the difference may reflect a firm's risk appetite and legal caution rather than a threshold in the food itself. Nothing on the pack distinguishes a use by that marks a genuine microbiological cliff from a use by chosen because it is the tidier position to defend. The consumer cannot read the reasoning off the label; they can only read the conclusion. That objection dents the idea that a use-by date is an objective safety number you can compare across brands. It does not dent the legal consequence. Once *use by* is printed, the FSA position and the sale restriction apply regardless of how conservatively the category was picked.

Let me be plain about where this account stops. The evidence behind it does not include Defra guidance on applying date labels; the Defra material available concerns country-of-origin labelling enforcement, which is a different question. The FSA wording quoted here comes from reporting of the agency's consumer pages, not from its business guidance. And I cannot tell you how often anyone checks the file. Whether regulators routinely audit shelf-life evidence, or only ask to see it after a complaint or an incident, is not established by what I have. So this is an account of what an operator is required to be able to show, not a claim about how frequently they are asked to show it.

Go back to the pot. The two words in front of the number are not a softer and a harder way of saying the same thing. They are the visible output of a decision taken before the product existed in a shop, by a company, using testing it commissioned, under storage conditions it specified. You can check that much yourself in about four seconds: read the phrase, then read the storage line underneath it, and notice that the second one is a condition on the first. What you cannot check, standing in the aisle, is how much room the operator left itself. That part stays in the file.
