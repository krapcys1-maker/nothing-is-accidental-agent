# The Beg Button Lie: Your Wait Is Defined as Leftover Car Green

There is a button on the pole. It has a shape designed to be pressed, sometimes an arrow, sometimes a small light that acknowledges the press. And there is a documented setting, called recall mode, in which pedestrian crossings are already part of the signal cycle and pressing the button quite literally changes nothing. A New York City DOT spokeswoman put the working share of the city's crosswalk buttons at about 9 percent. Reporting on Dallas found nearly all of that city's 200 traffic signals running on a pre-timed basis, so hitting the crosswalk buttons did nothing there either.

That is enough to retire the button as an explanation for anything. It is not the mechanism. It is a hand-shaped object attached to a machine that, in these configurations, has already decided.

The mechanism is written down, in a definition, inside a federal pedestrian research document, and it is the part worth carrying around. In that delay model, the pedestrian's waiting time is not a number someone types in. It is a sum of other people's time. Pedestrian effective red time is defined as flashing DON'T WALK time during the vehicle red indication, plus flashing DON'T WALK time during a "yield to peds" indication, plus vehicle green time. Three terms. One of them is the entire green phase for cars.

Read that definition as arithmetic rather than as policy. If the vehicle green in a cycle gets longer, the pedestrian's effective red gets longer by exactly the same amount, because vehicle green is a term inside it. No one has to decide to make walkers wait. The walker's red is the residual, the leftover shape around a green that was allocated for other reasons. Whatever those reasons are in a given city, the model does not require malice to produce a long wait. It only requires the green to grow.

I want to be careful here, because this is where the story usually inflates. The formula tells you how pedestrian delay is computed. It does not tell you why a particular intersection on a particular street holds a particular green length, and the evidence in front of me does not establish that.

The second half of the mechanism is about what gets measured. FHWA researchers recommended that the Highway Capacity Manual, the reference document engineers use to score how well an intersection performs, adopt pedestrian delay as a primary measure of effectiveness for pedestrian street corner analysis. That is a recommendation, and my reading of it is straightforward: you do not recommend that a manual adopt a measure as primary if it already was. Something else was occupying that slot. The inference is mine, offered as interpretation, and I will not dress it up as more. Nothing in the evidence I have says signal software legally inherits a vehicle-delay metric, or that any rule instructs anyone to treat a driver's ten seconds and a walker's ninety as interchangeable units. If you have seen that claim made confidently, these documents do not support it.

What the recommended criteria do contain is more interesting than a conspiracy. The proposed level-of-service scale for signalized crossing delay runs from under 10 seconds of average delay per pedestrian at the top to over 60 seconds at the bottom. Alongside each band sits a second column: likelihood of pedestrian noncompliance, rising from low as the delay grows. The scale anticipates that as waits lengthen, people stop obeying. The behaviour we moralise about at street level is written into the engineering table as a predictable output of delay. The person crossing against the signal is not defeating the system's model. They are confirming it.

That, to me, is the real cost, and it is not primarily measured in seconds. A pre-timed or recall-mode signal gives the pedestrian an interface that produces no effect and no information. Press it and the WALK arrives on schedule. Press it twice and the WALK arrives on schedule. Skip it entirely and the WALK arrives on schedule. The felt wait is completely decoupled from the pressed button, which means the wait cannot be read as an allocation, only as weather. Nobody stands at a corner concluding that vehicle green is a term in their own effective red. They conclude that the light is slow today.

The strongest objection cuts a real piece out of this. On a fixed-time signal the WALK message appears automatically every cycle, so an inert button does not lengthen anyone's wait by a single second. It takes away agency and feedback, not green time. Those are different injuries, and conflating them makes the case weaker than it needs to be. Current federal guidance also promotes multimodal, objectives-based timing and pedestrian priority strategies, which means a vehicle-centred measure of effectiveness is better described as drift in practice than as something inherited and locked.

Let me state the boundaries once. The button figures I have describe two cities at the moment they were reported, not a national survey, and the underlying counts behind the New York share are reported inconsistently enough that I have declined to build on them. I have no quantified comparison between a long pedestrian wait and a coordinated vehicle green wave. Whether coordination along an arterial is the specific cause of long pedestrian waits is not documented in this evidence, and I am not asserting it. The delay definition is a modelling convention from a federal pedestrian research document, and the level-of-service bands are recommended criteria, not a measurement of your corner.

Which still leaves you something to check, and it does not require any of those documents. Stand at a crossing with a phone timer and take three numbers: the steady WALK, the flashing DON'T WALK, and the green for the traffic beside you. Add the last two. By the model's own definition, that sum is your effective red, and the largest term in it belongs to the cars. Then do the cheaper test. Cross once having pressed the button and once having ignored it, and see whether the WALK arrives the same way both times. If it does, you have learned that the button was never the variable, and that the thing you were waiting on was a number set for someone else, sitting inside a formula with your name on it.
